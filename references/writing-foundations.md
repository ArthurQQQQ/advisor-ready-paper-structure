## Organize what the reader needs to learn

Build the piece around one organizing insight that the reader can remember and explain. In a method paper, this is the general idea that makes the approach address the problem. In an analysis paper, it is the central finding or explanatory question. Connect the supporting contributions to that thread.

Introduce unfamiliar terms before relying on them. Give an overview before a sequence of technical steps. At a transition, carry a concept from the preceding sentence or paragraph into the next one when it helps the reader follow the argument. Each step should have a clear purpose in the whole.

Scale this approach to the requested task. A paragraph edit can improve its local purpose and transition. A short message needs the reader's context and a direct point. A paper outline can make the full argument explicit.

## Introduction

Develop these five answers, checking the closest prior work as the framing evolves.

1. **Problem.** Introduce the task and the terminology needed by a reader new to the area, then state the precise problem and its boundaries.
2. **Value.** Explain who faces the problem and why solving or understanding it matters. Describe practical, scientific or societal consequences at the scope the evidence supports.
3. **Challenges.** Explain what makes the problem hard, and how each challenge creates a requirement for the approach or study.
4. **Closest prior attempts.** Use one or two sentences per directly relevant paper to explain its approach, what it establishes and the particular limitation or unanswered question that motivates this work. Keep this introduction comparison focused. Develop broader literature groupings in Related Work.
5. **Core idea.** Explain the conceptual insight and why it should address the difficulty. Keep this explanation independent of implementation details, evaluation numbers and dataset-specific choices. Put concrete identities, settings and reproducibility details in the relevant later sections.

After the five answers, give a compact experiment or study preview. Name the actual scope and main supported findings at a high level, with only completed evidence stated as results. Numbers used to describe model families, datasets or outcomes must come from the performed work.

Finish with a bullet list of contributions when appropriate to the paper. Each item should already be motivated or explained in the preceding introduction. The list consolidates the contribution rather than introducing a new result, idea or claim.

Revisit these answers during the research discussion, then choose the organizing paper type from the main contribution and available evidence before writing.

## Method or study design

Open with the general idea and a short overview of the inputs, steps and output. Before each important step, explain its intuition and connection to the previous step in words, then introduce its mathematics or implementation.

Use the established method name for what the system computes, assumes or outputs. Use We or I for the authors' research actions, observations and hypotheses when that matches the authorial voice. Keep these roles clear.

Choose intuitive, compact mathematical symbols, often single letters where the discipline permits. Define them on first use and preserve established notation and distinctions. Keep the names of the method, components, data and quantities stable throughout the paper.

An analysis paper uses this section to explain the study design, measurements, comparisons, controls and assumptions. Its organizing insight determines which details the reader needs to interpret the findings.

## Experiments and findings

Use the following sequence for each experiment or analysis, adapting paragraph boundaries to the material.

1. State the question or goal of the experiment.
2. Point to the relevant table or figure.
3. Explain how to read it, including relevant rows, columns, methods, metrics, axes, legend or color encoding.
4. State a direct observation and its corresponding interpretation or reason. Keep them close enough that the reader can tell which observation supports which explanation.
5. Close with the supported implication for the proposed method, study question or central finding.

A performance difference supports the measured comparison under its stated conditions. A causal or mechanistic explanation needs the design and evidence that establish it. The interpretation should explain the observation just presented.

When the evidence shows a benefit for the proposed method, end by explaining that benefit. When it shows a limitation, failure or unresolved comparison, close with that supported implication and its consequence for the research. Preserve material negative and inconclusive findings.

## Figures, tables and captions

Make a figure or table understandable to a reader who encounters it before the surrounding prose. Include descriptive internal labels and a caption that identifies the comparison, essential conditions, metrics and main point. Explain abbreviations, units, axes and visual encodings where needed. The body text still introduces how the display supports the argument.

For a method paper, a useful opening figure illustrates the problem and core insight. A following figure can summarize the method. Choose placement and numbering from the actual manuscript. For an analysis paper, prioritize the study design or decisive finding when those displays best explain the contribution.

Apply the actual venue's format to the appendix and supplement as well as the main text. Check affected figures, tables, widths and cross-references against the supplied or current applicable template.

When the author wants generated concept or method diagrams, use the optional [figure-style selection](figure-styles.md). Compare the same content in different visual directions, record the author's selection and keep the paper's figures consistent. The style controls presentation; measured results are plotted from their source data.

## Conclusion

Answer the opening question with the completed work and supported insight. Then develop a concrete implication, assumption, limitation or future direction that follows from that evidence and connects the work to its field.

Keep the conclusion's claim at the same scope as the results. Use future work to address a real open question or boundary.

## Sentence-level writing

- Give each sentence one central idea. Split long sentences that join independent claims with rhetorical dashes, semicolons or explanatory colons. Preserve notation, code, citations, quotations and required formatting where their punctuation carries meaning.
- Use one stable term for one thing. Keep the selected model or method name and distinguish a component from the whole system. Avoid cycling through model, system and framework as stylistic substitutes for the same referent.
- Prefer direct statements with a clear actor. In research prose, We or I can describe what the authors study, model, test, observe or hypothesize. Use the named method when the method performs an operation.
- Connect sentences through their concepts. Let paragraph openings pick up the preceding idea when that makes the transition natural.
- Use metaphors sparingly and only when they clarify an important point. Keep repeated punchy lines and rhetorical contrasts out of routine explanation.
- Remove intensifiers such as exactly, alone or same when they only add emphasis. Retain them when they specify a mathematical equality, an isolated intervention, a shared evaluation condition or another substantive distinction.
- In a style-only edit, preserve the author's meaning, terminology, facts, qualifications and stance. Apply changes to expression within that scope.

For short correspondence or general prose, use the reader, organizing insight, stable terms, transitions and sentence-level principles at the scale of the task. Scientific section structures apply where the requested artifact calls for them.
